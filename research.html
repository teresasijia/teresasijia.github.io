Are Ambiguity Attitudes Transferable? A Test Using DOSE

with Alexander L. Brown Draft upcoming

Abstract
We examine whether model-specific ambiguity-attitude parameters are stable across different ambiguous situations and can therefore support out-of-sample prediction. The smooth ambiguity model and modified Hurwicz expected utility allow perceived uncertainty, a belief component, to account for behavioral differences across situations, whereas Source Theory does not. The former models are therefore expected to yield ambiguity-attitude parameters that are more stable and transferable. Our first study provides initial support for this prediction: estimated ambiguity attitudes under Source Theory change substantially across situations. Moreover, even when perceived uncertainty is proxied under a Bayesian-updating assumption, the estimated parameters under the smooth ambiguity model and modified Hurwicz expected utility are more stable. To provide a more complete test, the second study adapts Dynamically Optimized Sequential Experimentation (DOSE) to separately estimate individual-level second-order beliefs, which capture perceived uncertainty, and ambiguity attitudes. We then compare the models' out-of-sample predictions across the two situations. Modified Hurwicz expected utility outperforms the other two models, while the smooth ambiguity model does not outperform Source Theory. This shows that accounting for perceived uncertainty does not by itself guarantee better predictive performance. Performance also depends critically on how a model incorporates that belief component.

Control and Equity Tradeoff in a PartnershipRevise and resubmit, Journal of Industrial Economics

SSRN

Abstract
This paper studies control reallocation in a partnership, where the partners hold asymmetric information about whose expertise should direct the firm. Using a mechanism design approach, it characterizes the first- and second-best mechanisms that incorporate equity transfers as part of the design. The analysis provides novel insights into how the interdependence between partners' expertise shapes the optimal equity transfer. It shows that both partial and full equity transfers may emerge as part of the optimal mechanism. Specifically, with a control shift, both the first- and second-best solutions may prescribe full equity transfer under substitute expertise, but minimal equity transfer under complementary expertise.
Bayesian Persuasion with Unknown Types of Receivers: An Experimental Investigation

with Jinliang Liu · SSRN

Abstract
Canonical models of Bayesian persuasion assume that the sender knows the receiver's preferences when designing information. In many real-world settings, the sender knows only how common each type of preference is, not which type of receiver she will face. We study Bayesian persuasion with such type uncertainty in a laboratory experiment. By manipulating the receiver's payoff structure, we create two types: one is harder to persuade, while the other is easier to persuade because its payoff makes the sender-preferred action more attractive. Across two between-subjects treatments, we vary only the receiver-type distribution, so that either the hard-to-persuade type or the easier-to-persuade type is more common. We find that senders adjust their plans in the direction predicted by theory: when the easier-to-persuade type is more common, they choose less informative plans. The adjustment, however, is smaller than the theoretical benchmark predicts. We argue that this smaller magnitude reflects a rational response rather than a mere underreaction: the easier-to-persuade type turns out harder to persuade than theory predicts, and senders appear both to anticipate this in their beliefs and to react by supplying more information when that type is more common.
Asymmetric Information Undermines Coasian Bargaining in Common (Oil) Pool Resources

with Alexander L. Brown, Catherine Eckel and Steven Wiggins Draft on request

Abstract
Oil field unitization is a unique, high-stakes economic problem involving multilateral bargaining among many agents with certain positive surplus from agreement. We theoretically characterize and experimentally test the problem under three information structures: complete information, symmetric incomplete information, and asymmetric information in the form of private information about holdings. Our cooperative bargaining theoretical framework suggests that agreement is always possible under both symmetric information structures but may fail under asymmetric information. Conditional on agreement, the returns to private holdings should be highest under asymmetric information, and weakly higher under complete information than under symmetric incomplete information. Experiments confirm that agreement rates are lowest under asymmetric information and similar under both symmetric information structures. However, returns to private holdings are discounted similarly under asymmetric and symmetric information, with both falling below those under complete information. It appears that subjects consistently impose norms that discount holdings that are not commonly known with certainty. We re-examine evidence from failed unitization attempts in the field and find they are consistent with this explanation.
Probabilistic and Financial Literacy Impact on Ambiguity Attitudes towards Financial Instruments

with Giovanni Burro and Alessandro Castagnetti Draft upcoming

Abstract
We examine the impact of standard and probabilistic financial literacy on ambiguity attitudes towards real-world financial assets. In a laboratory experiment, we provide subjects with either no literacy; only standard financial literacy; or we couple this with probabilistic literacy. We then elicit ambiguity attitudes for the stock of a locally well-known company and for Bitcoin. With respect to the no literacy group, we find that probabilistic literacy decreases ambiguity aversion towards both assets, with the effect concentrated among those who deemed the treatment most useful. Probabilistic literacy increases (decreases) ambiguity insensitivity towards the stock (Bitcoin). Standard financial literacy does not move ambiguity attitudes for any assets. Probabilistic literacy leads to lower noise in choices with respect to the other two treatment arms. Our results highlight the importance of probabilistic reasoning in financial literacy and the source dependence of ambiguity attitudes and changes in them.
